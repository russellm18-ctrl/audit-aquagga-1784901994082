## Atmospheric Reality Gap

Your copy leads with "Award Winning PFAS Destruction" backed by seven federal agencies — DOD, EPA, DOE, DARPA, FAA, NSF, AFWERX. That credential stack positions Aquagga as category-defining infrastructure technology. The trust ask from a procurement officer at a semiconductor fab or a DoD remediation contractor is enormous: multi-year contracts, regulatory exposure, capital equipment decisions.

The image infrastructure tells a different story. Two of your market-section images carry filenames `66f1a44c985a458e96c402f8_IMG_0986.avif` and `69d5738e5ff51c8a9958169b_IMG_3053.jpg` — raw phone-capture filenames routed through a Webflow CDN hash, unchanged. Those filenames are the digital equivalent of handing a procurement officer a napkin sketch instead of a spec sheet. They signal that no professional production process touched these assets. The scan confirmed 42 content images on the homepage; the forensics cannot tell me what those images look like, and I won't invent that. What the forensics can tell you is this: zero of 42 carry alt text, which means there is no textual layer reinforcing what those images are supposed to prove. The visual evidence your copy promises is running on infrastructure that communicates amateur-hour to anyone who looks beneath the surface — and enterprise buyers do look.

The trust cost: a buyer who Googles competitors after landing on your page encounters organizations whose image assets are named, described, and staged to match the contract size they're pursuing. Your credential copy raises the bar; your image infrastructure doesn't clear it.

## Visual Narrative Coherence

Your #1 Google ranking for "Aquagga" means warm traffic arrives — procurement teams, DoD contractors, environmental engineers — and the homepage navigation routes them to solution pages (Colt, Steed, Stampede series), market verticals, and case studies. The 25-link audit returned zero broken links, which is operationally clean.

The fracture is structural, not mechanical. The Colt, Steed, and Stampede product sections each carry identical copy: "Available for lease, purchase, or demonstration projects." Three product lines, three price points, three distinct use-case profiles — differentiated by a single image each and no descriptive text that distinguishes them visually or functionally in the scroll. A high-value buyer comparing vendors needs to see the technology operating in context. The image filenames for two of your four market sections suggest field photography that was never purpose-shot for a buyer audience. The third market section image (`687ae0f16b4b715f1922210b_20250514_065304.jpg`) carries a datestamp filename from May 14, 2025 — again, unprocessed capture metadata in a production environment.

What a serious vendor comparison reveals: your affiliations section is dense with institutional logos (WEF, Colorado School of Mines, DARPA), which is legitimate proof. But proof without visual documentation of the technology in operation leaves the buyer assembling a mental picture from logos alone. That gap is where competitors who look the part collect the RFQ.

## Metadata and Conversion Gaps

The single most expensive findability failure: `og:image` is missing entirely. Every time a link to aquagga.com is shared in a Slack channel, a procurement email, or a LinkedIn post, the preview renders as a blank tile. In enterprise sales, that link gets forwarded to three people before anyone clicks it. A blank og:image preview is a credibility tax on every share your advocates generate.

Your page title — "Aquagga - PFAS Destruction Units" at 32 characters — leaves roughly 30 characters of indexable real estate unused. Competitors targeting "PFAS destruction technology" or "PFAS remediation equipment" can claim that keyword territory in their titles while yours stops short.

The alt-text situation is the compounding failure: 42 content images, 100% missing alt text. Search engines cannot index what your images depict. For a company whose differentiation lives in visual proof of technology — unit deployments, field operations, laboratory validation — every untagged image is a page that search cannot read and a screen reader that returns nothing to an accessibility-conscious government procurement process.

Three fixable infrastructure failures, zero of which require a new photo shoot to address — but all three become significantly more expensive to ignore once a competitor with better image infrastructure starts ranking for the terms your technology actually owns.
